'Outlier' generally means odd one-out,

Outlier can effect the decision of KNN algorithm, if 'k' is not choosen appropriately.

⚠️

Some examples of what 'K' values shouldn't be taken

Critical edge cases to avoid when choosing K in binary classification

1. Even numbers as K Tie Deadlock

In a 2-class problem (Cats vs. Dogs), an even number like K = 2 or K = 4 can cause an equal tie (2 Cats vs. 2 Dogs). The model gets stuck in a 50-50 deadlock and has to guess or flip a coin!

⚠️ Risk: 50-50 ties force random guessing or coin tosses.
2. K = 1 (Single Neighbor) Overfitting

Asks only 1 closest pet. If standing near that 1 sneaky cat outlier sitting by the dogs, the model gets tricked 100% of the time! It memorizes pure noise instead of the real neighborhood.

🚨 1 sneaky cat outlier commands 100% of the decision.
3. K = N (K = 25: All Pets) Underfitting

Asks all N = 25 pets across the entire dataset! It completely ignores who is close by and simply counts whoever has more total pets (13 Cats vs. 12 Dogs → predicts Cat everywhere).

⚠️ Neighborhood location is completely lost; over-smoothed.

The "Elbow Method" Explained Simply

📉 1. What is the "Elbow Method"?

Instead of guessing K blindly, we test different values of K and plot the Validation Error Rate on a curve.

As K increases, the error drops sharply from noise, reaches a lowest point, and then climbs back up as neighborhoods blur. That turning dip is called the "Elbow"!

2. Why Point K = 5 on the Graph?
Lowest Error (4.0%)

On the graph, K = 5 sits at the very bottom of the error valley (only 4.0% error):

  • ✓ Outvotes the Outlier: Asking 5 neighbors means the 4 honest dogs easily overrule the 1 sneaky cat (4-to-1 majority).
  • ✓ Preserves Local Boundaries: Asking 5 pets stays within the true local cluster, unlike large K values which poll the whole dataset.
The Solution • Optimal Best Practices

The Solution: How to Choose K in Practice (K = 5)

Now that we have tested why Even K, K = 1, and K = 25 fail, and seen where the Elbow Valley lands, here is how we pick the optimal solution (K = 5) using 4 practical rules:

√N
Foundational Heuristic

The √N Golden Baseline

Our Dataset: N = 25 → K = √25 = 5 ★

Choosing k = √n perfectly balances the bias-variance trade-off by ensuring the number of neighbors grows large enough to smooth out random noise (lowering variance) but small enough relative to the total dataset size to keep predictions localized (lowering bias).

This rule of thumb directly stems from the foundational mathematical criteria proposed by the creators of the algorithm, Evelyn Fix and Joseph Hodges (1951). They proved that for KNN to remain stable as data grows, the number of neighbors must approach infinity while its ratio to the dataset size approaches zero—a condition the square root function naturally satisfies.
